## S1E2-E3 notes [T]

Method on new participants. Angela (44, wants children "in a marriage, with a man", rural, off-grid, wary of wireless radiation and Halloween as satanic).

- **Build the stage from the subject's stated ideal, then audit it for realism.** He relocates her from city to rural Oregon because she said she would not raise a child in a city. Later he suspects she wants "a fun vacation in her dream home" instead of an accurate simulation, and says so in narration: the rehearsal's purpose is accuracy, not pleasure. He also admits "I hadn't intended for this to be easy."
- **Observation pattern: log everything, concede and accommodate, but note the gap.** He nods along to the beliefs (Satanism on Halloween, Google controlled by the Devil, "keyword search") with soft, non-confrontational questions ("Oh, maybe... did you do a keyword search?"), lets her talk herself into the claim, and never argues. His only pushback is mild ("maybe let's not talk about this around the kids").
- **Robin as a test of the simulation's fidelity.** The date partner is a numerology-minded Christian who sees "88" and "33" everywhere. Fielder rides along "to do my due diligence on behalf of the parents" (the parents of the child actors) and gently asks for realism ("Why do you think so much more about numbers than... the real-life experiences?"). The scene is left to run uncut when Robin confronts a stranger; Fielder asks afterwards what that was about and takes the answer straight.
- **Consent choreography.** Each change goes to the child actors' real parents for sign-off (lavender oil on soles of feet; phone call asking permission before Fielder enters the house as "father"). On the call he offers an opt-out twice ("if you're feeling even the slightest bit uncomfortable, I would like to opt out") but frames the continuing as "groundbreaking" and the parent answers warmly. To Angela, he says "I know I'm in a position of power", asks her to be honest, and then accepts a casual "why not?" as consent. Pattern: scrupulous about asking, unsure whether asking can ever fully work when the asker holds the camera.
- **Rule-breaking when the simulation would otherwise fail.** Crew may not do childcare, yet he babysits "just this once" so Angela can go on the date. Same logic: the experiment outranks his own rule.
- **Sleeping night-owl bit.** The man hired to make the robot baby cry falls asleep twice; he confronts him ("Didn't you describe yourself as a night owl?") and re-sets the setup. Fielder treats participant failures as production bugs to patch.
- **Narration style [T].** Short declaratives; instruction-manual tone about absurd logistics ("Each week Adam will grow three years, and for each age multiple child actors will be needed"); small confessions slipped in as asides ("I transported a fake bar across the country... because I didn't want to throw it away", "it's nice to have something familiar around"). The asides humanize him and quietly set up the theme of his own loneliness.
- **Thesis stated as lesson learned (S1E3).** "I've recently come to realize I've been neglecting one key component of every crucial life event. Feelings." He observes that his earlier subject mastered words and choreography, then froze at the real confrontation, so the rehearsal missed the feeling. This is the central S1 method upgrade: rehearse stakes and emotion, not only lines.
- **Escalating deception to engineer feeling.** For Patrick (whose brother controls an inheritance) he secretly stages a fake grandfather with buried gold and a death, so Patrick will have the same stakes and emotions. He asks Patrick to change the old man's diaper-equivalent care, then announces the "death" to him. Patrick later breaks down in the rehearsal and calls the actor-brother "brother", then vanishes before the real conversation (goes to an amusement park, phone dead). Narration: "Maybe for some, the rehearsal itself is enough." Failure is reported flatly and re-framed as a possible success.
- **Cultural/tone management.** When Patrick uses an antisemitic stereotype in rehearsal, Fielder neither scolds nor ignores; he calls the problem "calibrate" ("to me it's like... that's an anti-Semitic stereotype") then defers to realism ("I don't want you to use language that wouldn't be effective"). Candor with minimal moralising.
- **Self-exposure at the end of S1E3.** After helping others generate feelings he cannot "so easily create... for myself". Envy of others who "can just believe" and "immerse themselves... with so little effort". Closing line: "even when you think of everything, there will always be things you forget."

## S2 argument and escalation [R unless marked]

**Problem to method.**
- Real problem (Fielder's own account [I], Rolling Stone): about 20 years of fascination with commercial aviation disasters. Investigators fix the technical causes; the human cause recurs: one pilot sees a problem, the other sees it differently, and the one with the better path is afraid to speak. He used to bring this up as a dinner-conversation topic. Pitched to HBO's Amy Gravitt in 2023 with footage of himself in early flight lessons.
- Method: if the first-officer problem is social discomfort with authority, apply the Season 1 method (rehearse the uncomfortable interaction, with actors) to pilots. Stage 1 premise per The Ringer: crashes are driven by co-pilots' fear to challenge captains. Fielder's thesis is stated as a hypothesis the series then tests.
- E1 (CJR, Rolling Stone): opens with reenacted cockpit audio of real crashes (the "joke vibe" captain whose co-pilot "can't see" before the crash). Meets ex-NTSB member John Goglia: "No jokes. The goal is to earn his trust." Then notes more than ten minutes passed with "zero laughs" and says so aloud; in the interview he says this is his insecurity: if the thing is not funny for long enough, he feels he has to say it out loud.
- E2 ("Star Potential") [R: Ringer, Slate, CJR]: Wings of Voice, a fake singing competition hosted by pilots, to train criticism and rejection gently and to produce public attention. Contestants who feel off-put by him lead him to obsess about likability. The "sincerity is overrated" voice-over appears here. A contestant (Lana Love) later says she spent over $5,000 on travel for auditions (Slate).
- E3 "Pilot's Code" [R: Ringer]: after cloned-dog experiments (pets/animals as a way of testing identity and nurture), he picks Sully's first officer Jeff Skiles to illustrate "healthy" cockpit communication. His reasoning: if captains had Sully's instincts "it's possible the entire aviation issue I've been obsessed with would vanish overnight". Method: live Sully's life from birth: shaved head and body, diaper, giant puppet mother who reads Charlotte's Web and breastfeeds (a milk geyser; "Fuck"), Sully's father gives toy plane, the teenage-Sully date, compartmentalising desire and flight. Climax: a theory that Sully's iPod and Evanescence's "Bring Me to Life" let him get in touch with his feelings and ask for help. Ringer calls this the series' typical "hall of mirrors": the joke is also a serious critique of biopics that reduce a life to defining moments. Ends with a montage of pilots talking to his staff in a "therapy-but-not-therapy" setting (Gravitt in the interview speaks about it).
- E4 "Kissme" [R: TV Obsessive, Consequence, Slate]: actors posing as crew start personal conversations with pilots, with the narration reassuring the FAA this is "definitely not therapy". Focus on a socially awkward first officer, Colin, and dating. New technique "The Pack": actors mimic Colin's words and moves, sourced from a nature documentary about animals hunting in packs for confidence. He asks actors if any are attracted to Colin, says he cannot ethically pressure anyone, but will not stand in the way; Emma ends up dating Colin. He also runs five fake Colin/Emma couples in a replica apartment; they make out quickly while Colin only gets a peck; partners of actors watch calmly. Insight: acting appears to drain social risk ("it's not real real... but it's happening when it's happening", Slate quoting an actress). Result: the "Captain Powers / Jennifer Kissme" character exercise.
- E4 end / E5 "Washington" [R]: Goglia suggests taking the idea to Congress. First, a rehearsal of the congressional hearing with actors as the subcommittee. Goglia coaches him to stop saying current practices are "terrible". Fielder watches comedians who testified, sees they open with a joke, then rehearses getting laughs, tells actors it is OK to laugh, then tells them not to over-laugh (a joke on his misjudging his own power as director). WBD lobbyists' request to Rep. Sam Graves is declined; he googles himself and lists his self-embarrassments. He notices articles about the show and autism, claims it was never his intent (feigned ignorance, per TV Obsessive), and sees Rep. Steve Cohen sits on both the aviation subcommittee and the Autism Caucus. Visits the Center for Autism and Related Disorders (Dr. Doreen Granpeesheh); she shows an eyes-reading test; he lets her use the Houston airport replica for autistic children and gets a board seat. In the real meeting with Cohen he does not rehearse ("he definitely does not need to"), reads his points, and proposes "Captain Allears / First Officer Blunt", assigned character names that pilots use to introduce themselves. Cohen is unconvinced.
- E6 "My Controls" [R: TV Obsessive, Consequence, Ringer, Slate, IndieWire]: reveal that he had been training as a pilot for years ("TWO YEARS EARLIER", Slate). Training narrative: could not land, instructor repeatedly took over, 120 hours when most manage it in about a dozen (TV Obsessive), watched another student crash, then spent months doing a chair "imagine it" exercise before returning. Plan: fly a 737 with actor passengers and a real first officer, exploiting two loopholes (not enough hours for commercial, not allowed with paying passengers; actors make it legal). Month of simulator training in Henderson, NV; co-pilot Aaron (5,000+ hours, a Wings of Voice participant with Hollywood aspirations) chosen because he might fear a "captain" in entertainment. Medical form asks about "anxiety, etc."; brain scan (fMRI) offered by Dr. Jordan; results not ready in time; online pilots say diagnosis risks a license; he ticks "no" based on current knowledge. Flight goes fine; Aaron is so relaxed that there is nothing to resolve, he keeps asking if all is OK, then stops. Wings of Voice winner Isabella sings "Bring Me to Life" in the airport replica. A voicemail with results arrives; he deletes it. Final voice-over: flying relocation flights for out-of-service 737s; "no one is allowed in the cockpit if there's something wrong" so "you must be fine".
- Aftermath [I/R]: the FAA statement disputing the thesis; on CNN he called the FAA "dumb" and joked about the "Miracle over the Mojave" (CJR). At THR event he says he would still testify "if they will have me".

**Escalation logic (pattern).** Each episode takes the previous episode's technique and moves it one step out of control: hearing the problem (E1) → manufacture a public (E2) → test whether a personality can be transplanted (E3, Sully) → test whether acting lowers social risk (E4) → ask power for a hearing (E5) → do the thing himself and leave the proof in a cockpit (E6). The series ends not with institutional change but with a private act and a deleted voicemail.

## S2 narration patterns [R, with flagged exceptions]

- **Deadpan procedural.** Ringer: voice-overs "deadpan to the point of sounding pained"; Slate: "his voice pitched somewhere between deadpan and simply dead". Heavy use of institutional language (hypothesis, loophole, "definitely not therapy", telling the FAA).
- **Thesis stated plainly, then undercut by the staging.** "If other captains could have the instincts of Sully... [it] would vanish overnight" (Ringer paraphrase) and "If even just the tiniest bit of Sully could become a part of me, it would all be worth it" (Ringer quote). The setup claims a sober causal chain; the method is absurd; the narrator never admits the gap.
- **Calling out the joke deficit aloud.** E1 "zero laughs" (CJR). He says in interview this is his insecurity: say it out loud when something is not funny (Rolling Stone [I]).
- **Setbacks as new instructions.** Congress declines, so he builds a fake Congress; hearing fails, so he joins an autism board to get access; Cohen unconvinced, so he flies the plane. Each setback is narrated as a logistical problem with a bigger solution.
- **Withholding / reveal control.** The pilot's license was withheld until the finale by edit choice [I: THR, Rolling Stone]; he says he was open about it with people on camera and chose in the edit to reserve it. In the finale, a "TWO YEARS EARLIER" card.
- **Legal-loophole framing.** "He notes that he's exploiting a couple of loopholes" (TV Obsessive). Honest about constraints; asks for the letter-of-the-law yes.
- **Aphorism about people.** "Sincerity is overrated" ... "punishing those who can't perform it as well as others" (Ringer quote of voice-over). Another: "I don't know what it means. But it's interesting." (Ringer, end of E3.) Closing: "if you're here, you must be fine" (Consequence/CJR).
- **Mirror-to-viewer effect.** The Ringer's Brian Phillips: the show makes the viewer feel social anxiety toward the show itself; "Watching Nathan Fielder perform Nathan Fielder has turned us into the Nathan Fielder of interpreting Nathan Fielder."

## Turning it on himself

- **Method-as-diagnosis.** Slate: the show cites an article on masking; Fielder watches pilot Mara'D socialise "effortlessly" from a corner of the replica of S1's first bar. Phillips (The Ringer) thinks the autism beat is staged ("a plot twist... coyly refusing to confirm or deny") while TV Obsessive notes his "feigned ignorance" can't be real for the real Fielder.
- **Rehearsal refused for the one meeting that mattered.** E5: he says he need not rehearse the Cohen meeting; reads his notes awkwardly (TV Obsessive). A man who rehearses everything claims he can function without it.
- **Slow student pilot.** Could not land after 120 hours, months of imagined practice in a chair (TV Obsessive); in Rolling Stone he says at the pitch (Feb 2023, six weeks in) he "couldn't land the plane", and the footage of his struggles was what he showed Gravitt. He also tells the cloud-incident story: he deferred to an instructor flying them into cloud, did not take the controls, a real-life instance of the thesis. Quote-ish: "I let the guy fly me into a cloud... and I didn't stop it." [I]
- **fMRI and the deleted voicemail.** He ticks "no", the flight happens, he deletes the voicemail while Isabella sings. Slate offers two readings: denial, or already knowing and following the pilots' advice to bury a diagnosis. Consequence reads it as "what he doesn't want to know about himself". In the Rolling Stone Q&A, asked what he's saying about himself: "I'm trying to... I don't know." [I]. He adds the autism angle was "an interesting way to describe" the acting and "true self" theme, that people judge him via an edited TV show and some things "seep through that you can't control" [I].
- **Final voice-over turned inward.** The logic "no one is allowed in the cockpit if there's something wrong with them" is a catch-22 for pilots and self-reassurance for him (CJR: "reverse-engineer the entire premise").
- **Fear of being boring/unliked.** Wings of Voice drives him to obsess over his likability (Ringer). Googles himself and lists the times he made a fool of himself (TV Obsessive).
- **Slate's frame:** the person most in need of Nathan's help is Nathan; the show may itself be masking: the aviation plane story is misdirection while he "sneaks a deeply personal story" (home-movie footage as an apprentice magician).

## Interaction and ethics

- **Consent as a ritual line.** Actors in the 737 and the Colin pack are told they can opt out and he "won't hold it against them" (TV Obsessive); in the pack he says he cannot ethically pressure anyone and then lightly invites attraction; in S1E2 he gets verbal consent from child actors' parents with an offer to opt out. Pattern: explicit opt-out language that is worded to sound warm and nearly impossible to refuse.
- **Fiction used as cover for real consequences.** Colin's real dates; Wings of Voice contestants spending real money (Slate: Lana Love, >$5,000, broke NDA); child attachment in S1 (Slate, CJR). Slate: Fielder "owns up to his deceptions in the end - or seems to".
- **Legal care taken for the flight.** IndieWire: co-pilot with 5,000+ hours, chase plane, aviation consultants credited (Steve Giordano, Robert Allen), insurance and lawyers. The "actors" status is a legal necessity, not just a gag. In the interview he says the 737 flight was not certain until "days before" [I: THR].
- **Subjects he protects.** In Rolling Stone he interrupts the interview to say the instructor in the cloud story should not reflect on the people who trained him (he says "the people who trained me to fly were really good") and he was nervous sending Episodes 3 and 6 to Gravitt because of her brother's death [I]. He offered not to do the season: "We don't have to do this season if you don't want to" (partly joking) [I].
- **Power awareness.** S1E2: tells Angela he knows he is "in a position of power" so she should answer honestly. S2E5: tells actors it's OK to laugh, then misjudges how his direction works; TV Obsessive says "The Rehearsal has played with how Fielder doesn't seem to understand his own power as the show's director."
- **Critics' ethics frames.** CJR cites New Yorker critic Richard Brody calling S1 "cruel" and "arrogant", and Naomi Fry's view of a "relentless solipsism" self-portrait. Ringer: the show is "ethically murky" depending on viewing angle (compassionate, cruel, cynical).

## What he says about S2 in interviews

All [I]; Rolling Stone (RS) unless marked THR (Hollywood Reporter) or CJR (quoted via CJR).

- Origin: close to 20 years of fascination with aviation disasters; investigators fix technical causes but "the human stuff seems to keep happening over and over again" (RS, paraphrase).
- Why become a pilot: insecure that he is "just a comedian"; wanted to tell pilots "Hey, I'm a pilot, too" and understand them from the inside (RS). Wanted to show he was taking it seriously (THR).
- The show is "about how much of your true self you show and how to compensate for that with other means while having the fear of what others might think of you." (RS, paraphrase of his statement.)
- On communication: "if you take any two random people and make them talk, it will be the most uncomfortable thing you've ever witnessed." Based on his Canadian Idol work pairing contestants on camera. (RS)
- On the tonal risk: tone "always ends up being different than I picture it"; he says "with every joke you're like, is this too dumb or is it not? And sometimes it's not dumb enough" (RS). Effort principle: if you try even harder at something dumb, people "can't even judge. I just need to watch because of the effort" (RS).
- On being taken seriously: wants "to keep making jokes while being taken somewhat seriously" which was "an uphill battle" (RS). Sweet spot: "the middle ground of something that is real but also something that is funny and entertaining" (CJR citing the Vulture interview).
- On the puppet scene: he really felt held ("did feel like a mom was holding me"); the milk was oat milk and came too fast (RS).
- On the autism thread: "an interesting way to describe what might be happening as it relates to the acting stuff, the ability to be your true self or not... that question of sincerity" (RS). Pressed on whether it's about himself: "I'm trying to... I don't know." On being evaluated through an edited show he says people tell him how he is and it is "interesting."
- On the flight: he says "I do it when I have time"; "when you don't fly all the time, it gets a little scary"; joking "I passed the test, so I must know what I'm doing" (THR).
- On Congress: "If I could get in front of the Congress panel, I would have done that and I will still do that, if they will have me." (THR). On the FAA: "They don't seem to know what they're talking about, truly." (THR); on CNN "That's dumb" / "They're dumb." (CJR).
- Reveal: edit choice, not a plot he hid from the people on camera (THR, RS).
- Also, in RS: asked if he would speak up now he is a pilot: "If I'm still junior to the other pilot, I think I would still defer." A candid statement that the problem he addresses lives in him.
- Fielder usually does not discuss process outside the show (IndieWire: HBO respects that wish).

## Contradictions

1. **Serious cause vs. absurd method.** Thesis insists on air safety, solution is diapers, giant puppets and Evanescence. Phillips: "presumably, Fielder doesn't actually want to write aviation safety policy?" CJR: unsure the issue merits a season, but he "might actually be sincere".
2. **"Don't need rehearsal" vs. 100% rehearsal.** E5 voiceover says he does not need to rehearse the Cohen meeting; the whole series says rehearsal is a tool he uses to cope.
3. **Plans a proof flight yet nothing to test.** The co-pilot he picked to be intimidated is relaxed; no conflict emerges; thesis is neither supported nor refuted (TV Obsessive: "not quite bring that question home").
4. **Honest-looking vs. withheld.** He says he was open on camera about being a pilot, yet the show presents reveal-style; "pretended ignorance" about autism (TV Obsessive, Phillips) vs. his in-interview ambiguity.
5. **Autism ambiguity.** Phillips thinks the autism thread is staged; Slate and Consequence read the deleted voicemail as authentic avoidance. The only direct attempt at an answer from him is "I don't know."
6. **Consent vs. design.** Offers opt-outs, but participants are paid actors or invested in a project they cannot see in full; a Wings of Voice contestant claims she was duped, signed NDAs (Slate).
7. **Thesis vs. outcome.** The FAA says it already mandates interpersonal training and sees no supporting data (via CJR); Congress did not act; the season's concrete outcomes are a deleted voicemail and a pilot job.
8. **Fear of the ethical vs. bigger stakes.** He tells Gravitt he'd cancel the season over her brother's death, then escalates to real lives in a real jet.
