## The rehearsal method

- Decomposition: the scary moment (a 12-year-old lie confessed to a friend) is broken into setting, props, people, sequence, and timing. He gathers intelligence on every variable: a laser-mapped replica of the apartment (via a fake gas-leak visit), a replica of the bar down to a balloon and tilted portrait, the trivia night's format (rounds, a break, pizza timing), and the target's personality via her blog and a staged interview with a lookalike actor.
- Actors as simulators: an actor studies the real person (the "primary") and plays them so the subject can run the encounter repeatedly. Tricia's double is built from her blog, a fake-interview where the actor is lied to and told a lie, and Kor's own descriptions. Subject-supplied insight is treated as data, but data gets re-tested (Kor predicted extreme rage; the double showed mild forgiveness, then later runs were deliberately made harsher).
- Branching: responses are logged into flowchart/decision-tree software ("chart the optimal path"); every branch gets a planned reply (table taken, interruption by server, flirtation misread, friend dominates conversation). Practice includes failure branches: the harshest run has the double sobbing, calling it unforgiveable, and bar patrons mocking him.
- Optimization loop: run, observe, adjust one variable (where to confess, when to order pizza, tiny sips so the server doesn't interrupt, confess after the last round before scores). Thirteen full run-throughs; "reset button" framing: the one place you cannot fail.
- Escalating fidelity: when something is unknown (the trivia questions), he reasons that leaving it to chance is itself an ethical choice, and justifies deception (posing as a blog founder to get the questions, then teaching answers on walks). Preparation expands to cover whatever stays uncertain.
- What preparation can do: it lowered Kor's anxiety, handled a literal surprise (table taken, solved by a prepared grief-story script), and the real confession went well, even deepening the friendship for 90 minutes beyond the plan.
- What it can't: the real person drifted off-script (Kor delayed, waited for pizza, then improvised); and the method's emotional side effects were not rehearsable. The ep1 ending flips: the revealed cheating makes Kor furious and the rehearsal's trick "ruins" his achievement; Nathan then pivots to an unprompted compliment in a prepared-seeming apology, which smooths it.
- In E5/E6 the method becomes a life-scale simulation (Adam aged up at three-year jumps via window swaps, a fake mailman, 16 cameras, a replica bar). Failures: Angela abandons the rehearsal when he is away, leaves, and tells him in effect that he will never feel anything real; a child actor (Remy) truly attaches to "pretend daddy."
- Post-mortem replay: in E6 he reruns the goodbye with colder behavior, older actors, no actors, a different mom, hoping to find the avoidable mistake. Nothing "fixes" it; the sequence implies you can't rehearse your way out of attachment, only into reflection.

## Observation patterns

- People's fears are usually about aftermath and social standing, not the act: Kor fears losing trivia status and friendship, and tries to cap the conversation at five minutes, planning an exit line.
- Avoidance has structure: he stalls in dozens of small ways (waiting for the pizza bell, joking first). Nathan notices that people use the environment as an excuse.
- Honesty is often received better than feared (Tricia forgives), but also people overestimate how much others punish them; Nathan connects this to a general trap: assuming what others think turns them into characters in your head (E4 closing).
- Actors and participants continuously perform for him; he notices the distance between smile and underlying feeling (Thomas's quiet discomfort).
- Conflict-avoidance as a pattern: he observes his own tendency to let a partner steer to avoid friction, and sees it repeating in the fake family.
- Children, once attached, collapse the frame: Remy's mother reports that he said his pretend daddy loves him. The observation is that kids do not treat "pretend" like adults do.

## Turning it on himself

- E1 and E4 narration opens with his awkwardness: he is bad at first meetings, jokes are risky, his personality unsettles people. He rehearses even trivial moments (greetings, shoes-off joke).
- E1: a bathroom joke misfires; he reconstructs the reason afterward, imagining Kor heard criticism rather than self-deprecation. Heavy self-audit of tiny moments is the pattern.
- E1: he stages vulnerability (a planned interruption so he need not share more) and wonders whether it read as real. He is aware he is manufacturing intimacy to extract information.
- E4: to check whether his acting class works he recreates it with other actors to relive it from a student's perspective, finds nothing wrong, then re-does day one as Thomas and discovers Thomas's discomfort and cameras-delight, i.e. his own method has blind spots. He then goes all in, living in Thomas's life.
- E4: his teaching exposes insecurity about acting theory; he fakes understanding.
- E5: he recognizes his conflict avoidance and uses the rehearsal to practice asking for what he wants, but delays for months. Later he lies to Angela through Adam (coaching the boy on cover stories), hiding Jewish lessons.
- E5: Angela's reproach that he stands "like a rock" and his reaction (shaky "yeah" to wanting to feel something) is the strongest self-exposure; narration voice goes quiet and short.
- E6: he admits practicing counseling because he fears falling short as a father, then notices he is "solving a puzzle of my own design" and not present with the child; his mind is elsewhere. Narration directly names the cost: "what on earth was I doing." He then redoes it in a loop, trying alternatives.
- Narration style on self: plain, confessional declaratives, little hedging; emotion carried by the visual and music rather than by adjectives.

## Narration & expression patterns

- Openings: cold open at a mundane, awkward moment, then immediately "I have been told my personality..." type self-disclosure, then a plain statement of the premise: this person will trust me with something big, and "if you plan for every variable, a happy outcome doesn't have to be left to chance." (E1). E4 opens in someone's kitchen with a leading interview question, the narration comes after. E5 opens with a bizarre child game before the plot.
- Sentence shapes: short, flat declaratives; "So I..." / "But..." pivots to turn plans into complications ("But as we kept talking, I began to understand the real reason..."); explanations of absurd steps in a bureaucratic, reasonable tone ("To save money it was suggested I use background actors...").
- The reveal pattern: plan stated confidently, shown working, then a one-line "something happened" or "but this was just laying the groundwork" before the complication.
- Rationalization as comedy: he gives a sincerely reasoned argument for ethically dubious steps ("maybe it's more unethical to leave things to chance").
- Ending forms: a reflective aphorism over music (E1 "maybe it's easiest to choose a path when you can live the future first"; E4 "when you assume what others think..."; E5 "we don't always get to choose what happens in life, but we do get to decide if we rehearse for it"; E6 "you may never be able to change what happened. But maybe with a new perspective, you could try to change yourself"). Ending in a moment of unresolved human warmth or discomfort rather than a punchline.
- Comic devices: deadpan seriousness about trivial specifics (balloon in the real bar), dropped bombshells inside neutral sentences ("my TV friends are leaving"), repetition-as-torture (rerunning the same line variants), reaction shots of other people's cringe.
- Music cues: tense/whimsical/somber music marks mood shifts; narration is often silent through the emotional peak.

## Interaction technique

- Rapport engineering: scripted warmth plus lab-tested jokes, long walks, shared activities that bond (skeet shooting with blanks so both are bad), reciprocal disclosure at planned moments.
- Questions: leading but mild, asks for permission to continue the pleasant tone ("this conversation is going well, right?"), asks the subject to predict the worst case to find the real stakes.
- With actors: instructs through assignments with ethical gloss ("if you're inaccurate you could ruin someone's life"), uses authority and escalation (live in the primary's home, take their job), and offers one-on-one coaching that students find flattering. Teacher persona is insecure but seen as caring.
- Consent: formal release documents are signed quickly under social pressure (E4: "everyone else is signing it, it must be fine"); participants are often deceived (fake gas company, fake blog, fake interviewee) and only told later. Reveals are staged as confessions ("there's something I need to come clean about") that sound like an apology, which often leads to his target absorbing the guilt.
- Pushback handling: when Angela objects to the scatological sketch he defends it as sketch-comedy logic then leaves; when Kor is furious he waits, then gives an unplanned compliment; when Remy's mother cries, he checks in and visits, then redoes the scene. He retreats to the plan instead of confronting emotion head-on.
- When things go wrong: he adds more scenarios or another round of rehearsal; sometimes he reviews footage (E5) to detect hidden behavior. He rarely admits the method itself is flawed; he blames his execution, until E5/E6 where he asks a participant what she wants.
- The child-actor boundary: he explicitly tells Remy that Nathan is not his dad and checks again with the family; but the framing shows how a consent process with a child fails to cover emotional attachments.

## Contradictions

- Control vs spontaneity: total control of the environment (13 run-throughs, replica sets) produced a moving unscripted outcome; the only success was Kor drifting off-plan.
- Empathy vs manipulation: he is trying to understand people (living their lives) while deceiving them, using fake blogs, bugged scenes, and implanted answers.
- Help vs exploitation: the rehearsal helps Kor, but it is also content for HBO; Kor says he is "on TV now" and feels the win is tainted; the show treats this as both comedy and moral problem.
- Rehearsal vs presence: preparing for fatherhood stops him from experiencing it (E6), and in E5 Angela calls out that he can't engage when real feelings appear.
- Sincerity vs stunt: apologies sound sincere and are also devices (apologizing for the trivia cheating, planting a compliment).
- Care for actors vs using their lives: he asks people to quit their routine, lie to strangers, and (in E4) fake personal tragedies, then reflects that the actors are making sacrifices for him.
- Humility vs authority: he calls himself socially awkward while running a studio, a bar, and a family as director.
