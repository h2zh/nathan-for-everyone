# Primary material: The Curse (2023-24), transcript analysis

Caveat up front: The Curse is scripted fiction (Fielder and Benny Safdie). Asher Siegel is not Fielder. The useful question is which themes Fielder chose to dramatize and how they rhyme with his non-fiction work. Evidence below comes from complete reads of three episodes. Text transcripts carry no framing, silence, or performance, so claims about "how discomfort is built" are limited to what dialogue and stage cues show.

## Themes

1. **Virtue as a production value, edited on request.** In E4 the network exec praises the couple's "thoughtful take" on eco-homes, then asks them to stop mentioning that residents were displaced, because viewers will not "engage with" it. Asher and Whitney immediately agree this changes nothing in their "real life" and that not everything has to be in the show. The same cut shows up in E9: Whitney has to soften the Pueblo governor's speech about road easements into a "clerical concern," and the Diné pottery scene is cut for the network. Goodness is kept only as long as it is photogenic, and the couple does the trimming themselves, ahead of the order.
2. **Good deeds done for the lens, and good deeds hidden from it.** Asher prepays a chiropractor visit for the neighbor Abshir and visits a Sikh service in E4, apparently with no camera; he also fakes warmth with Abshir's daughter and uses a mold test as a pretext. In E5, Whitney tells the shop staff to charge shoplifted goods to her credit card so nobody calls the police, then asks them not to confront anyone. In E9 she tips $350 on a cancelled massage. Charity is real money, and it is also a way to avoid the harder act (honesty, a conversation, an apology).
3. **Casting the community.** In E5, after Dennis and Lucinda refuse to sign a letter in support of the tribe, Whitney drops them, floats using Remi or the college girl, and settles on "anyone" who can "embody the values of the community. Like an avatar." Dougie says his earlier shows used no real buyers at all. Street-cast strangers are offered $50, then $200, told "no jokes, no negativity," and one is told to pretend to be a couple. A "buyer" (Cara, an artist) turns out to be performing, and Asher calls her "a performance artist." The community is a set of roles.
4. **Intention vs effect, defended by both sides.** In E9, Whitney's parents, who run low-income buildings, say evicted people "move in with us" and "everyone stays in Española." Whitney says they are slumlords. Her own project is accused of the same displacement by a fired crew member (Patrick), and she handles it by promising to "use my relationship connections" and offering a tip-sized kindness to the driver Phoebe rather than facing the charge. The pattern across all three episodes is that each party's moral self-image depends on the other party being worse.
5. **Control and the managed marriage.** Whitney schedules a "playful" pastry gag with Asher "because it needs to be in the dailies," a countdown clock forces "you love me, right?" (E9), and she films him in confessionals describing the marriage as one where "the real version of you completely ceases to exist." The marriage is run like a production.
6. **The curse as externalized guilt.** The "curse" is a child's TikTok trend, and Dougie claims he is cursed. In E9 Asher says there is no curse and "I am the problem." The shift from magical thinking to confession is the show's moral turn, but it comes right after Whitney's confessional humiliates him, so it also reads as a way to retake the moral high ground.
7. **Dignity and money.** The cheap pottery is $1,500 a piece with a bulk discount; the passive house is $850,000; the AC question (E5) exposes that the certification matters more to Whitney than whether the buyers can bear living there ("a thermos"). Buyers who refuse a political letter lose the house; the buyer who donates to causes and flies a flag she dislikes gets it.

## Persona echoes and self-critique

- **Awkward, earnest, over-literal.** Asher over-explains and retracts ("it's a non-legally binding letter of intent," E5), and ad-libs jokes that do not land ("Prison toilets don't have lids" is a buyer's line; Asher is the one pushed to stop comparing the house to a prison). He does bits at a customer's expense, then apologizes.
- **The apology as a move.** In E4 Asher apologizes to Dougie for "bullying" him at camp and Dougie says it never happened. The apology serves the apologizer. In E9 he confronts Bill with "I'm the whistleblower," then privately rehearses it as an abusive rant in the mirror. Sincerity and rage sit in the same person.
- **Cosplaying goodness.** In E9 Bill asks Asher, "Does this get exhausting? Cosplaying as a good man?" The show lets a hostile character say the thesis, and Asher does not have an answer. It is the clearest self-critique of the persona in the sample.
- **Where he is complicit, not innocent.** Asher helps steal the range from Vic's curb while hiding from a doorbell camera (E4); he gives Whitney a pretext to dump Dennis and Lucinda; he cuts a native-artifact shortcut into the story (E9, said by Whitney's father, attributed to Asher). The awkward nice guy is shown being useful to a project he half-believes in.
- **Whitney as co-author.** She is the manager of appearances, coaching Vic on "language," drafting the support letter, deciding who counts as a community. The two feed each other: he needs approval, she needs a supply of it.
- **Desire for the dignified outcome.** E9 ends with Asher vowing to be "all in," Whitney being told "the world will know Whitney Siegel," and a closing line that treats her as a "good girl." The vow is as much self-flattering as loving.

## How discomfort is built

(From dialogue and stage cues only.)

- **A conversation about X that is really about Y.** The AC question in E5 is about heat and also about whether the buyers believe the sales pitch; the support letter is about the tribe and also about loyalty. Characters keep talking about the surface issue past the point where it is believable.
- **Slow stacking of micro-failures.** Missing phone, lost hat, wrong discount, wrong couple in the wrong scene. Several scenes are left in the middle of the damage rather than resolved.
- **The game imported into intimacy.** The bowling countdown in E9 is a reality-TV device (a timer, an answer demanded) used on a spouse. Tonya's "no recording in between filming" and Remi's prank "I quit" scene in E5 do the same with the workplace.
- **Rehearsed self-presentation.** Characters keep asking for another take (a second option "where you don't touch your stomach") or reminding each other what to do with their face. People are seen managing how they will be seen.
- **Jargon collision.** "Holistic," "unceded land," and "community" share sentences with "closing," "release forms," and "letter of intent." Each vocabulary undercuts the other.
- **A scene that cuts away just as a cruelty lands.** The mirror rant to Bill in E9, the son's car and the beer in E4.

## Ethics of the camera

- Consent is purchasable and revisable. Release forms are re-issued when the network wants a "positive spin" (E9), and the artist is chased for a signature (E5: "kind of a rush").
- People are blocked into roles: one actor must go in with his real girlfriend; a baby is not "an actor" but the host wants "a family"; a stranger's child is staged for the shot.
- The show shows the editing room. Whitney and Asher watch the pilot in bed (E9), and the producer asks what to cut. Her real-life confession about the marriage is "put back in the cut," then the network says "they don't want any of this."
- The pottery scene is the sharpest example of using culture as a prop: the Diné and Pojoaque makers are named on camera, then cut. Asher is shown the cut footage not to learn something, but because Whitney needs a witness to his insensitivity.
- Contract clause in E9: the performer "may be exposed to ridicule, humiliation or condemnation" and be "portrayed in a false light." The show puts the rules of its own making (and, by extension, reality TV) in the script.
- Parallel to Fielder's non-fiction: Nathan For You and The Rehearsal also hire people to play themselves and ask whether the staging is exploitative or generous. The Curse takes the other side of the camera and shows the people running it as largely unable to see their own extraction, while still being sympathetic about it.

## Contradictions

- **The curse is both real and fake.** E4 plants an eerie coincidence (the missing chicken, the static after "I curse you"), and E9 has Asher deny any curse.
- **Sincere apologies that are also bids.** The earnest persona is both the show's moral center and its target.
- **Community language is sincere and instrumental at once.** Whitney does care about the town; she also manages the care for the camera. The show refuses to rank the two.
- **The marriage works because of the show, and the show is eroding it.** The camera exposes their problems, and the network's script keeps them together ("no show if there's no Asher").
- **Fielder's characteristic empathy vs critique.** The show invites sympathy for Asher while documenting that he is complicit.
- **Dissent comes from compromised people.** The most direct critics (Patrick, Bill, Whitney's parents, Vic) are also hypocritical, which keeps the audience from a clean position.
