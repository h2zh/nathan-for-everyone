# Primary material: The Curse (2023-24), transcript analysis

Caveat up front: The Curse is scripted fiction (Fielder and Benny Safdie). Asher Siegel is not Fielder. The useful question is which themes Fielder chose to dramatize and how they rhyme with his non-fiction work. Evidence below comes from sampled transcript passages, not full reads.

## Themes

1. **Performative virtue as a production value.** The couple's HGTV pilot, "Fliplanthropy," sells passive green homes and "incorporating our buyers into this blossoming cultural oasis." The network's notes ask for a positive spin, and the show cuts the displaced-residents material. Asher and Whitney agree this changes nothing in their "real life," and that having a show "brings awareness." Goodness is justified by the exposure it earns, and exposure is what they actually want.
2. **Intention vs effect.** The homes are built to help the community, yet in E9 an activist accuses them of displacing tenants. Whitney's defense is that those people "move in with us," and Asher is living in a unit he does not need in order to be a better neighbor. Good intent is claimed as proof of good outcome. The show keeps showing the outcome (the boarded storefronts, the displaced, the artist whose pottery is cut from the edit).
3. **Casting "the community."** In E5 the couple chooses a buyer who will "embody the values of the community," like an avatar. They want the buyers to look right, pay them to appear on camera, and tidy their makeup. Real people become symbols for a story the producers already hold.
4. **Control and reality-TV manipulation.** The producers (Dougie, Martha, Pascal) request retakes, alternate framings, release forms, and a pottery scene that is "not in the show." In E5 Whitney stages a fake walkout and "quits" to catch a crew member laughing at her. In E9 Asher is shown footage of himself and told to stage a reaction. The cameras decide what counts as an event.
5. **Insecurity and the need to be liked.** Asher apologizes constantly, over-corrects when criticized, asks "You love me, right?" under a countdown clock in E9, and confesses to a petty lie (the hundred dollars) as proof that he, not a curse, is "the problem." His self-flagellation is also a bid for reassurance.
6. **The curse as externalized guilt.** The "curse" gets treated as the thing to be afraid of, a viral TikTok trend, a child's trick. In E9 Asher says there is no curse and that he is the problem. That is a moral realization but it comes out as confession-as-performance, aimed at Whitney.
7. **Gentrification and the cheapness of repair.** In E4, buying a drunk teenager alcohol, bargaining a criminal record, a coffee shop on the new San Pedro Plaza, "no Vic" because Vic is too awkward for the show: social repair is always priced and always transactional.

## Persona echoes and self-critique

- **Awkward, earnest, over-literal.** Asher over-explains ("a non-legally binding letter of intent," not a "contract"), hedges, and then retracts. He is the hapless "nice guy" in the Nathan-for-You mode, but the show lets him be unlikable: he lies to Nala, misleads Whitney, and uses his own therapy-speak as a shield.
- **The earnest apology as a move.** His apology to Dougie in E4 ("I want to say I'm sorry for bullying you at camp") is rebuffed, "You never bullied me." The apology makes the apologizer feel absolved and does nothing for the other person. The same structure shows up in Fielder's work: the sincere gesture that serves the gesturer.
- **Desire for the dignified outcome.** Asher wants to be seen as good. Whitney wants to be seen as good and also to win. Neither is willing to be good invisibly. This is the show's sharpest criticism of the "caring, awkward guy" persona: the awkwardness is not innocence, it is a style of self-protection.
- **Where the show turns on the persona.** E9 ends in a spiraling confession and a vow to be "all in" that reads as both sincere and manipulative. The show suspends judgment but plants the doubt that the sincerity is the performance. This is a harder look at the Fielder voice than anything in Nathan for You, where the persona is always the straight man and the other people are the subjects.
- **Whitney as a counterweight.** She is the one who can be cruel, controlling, and magnetic, and she reframes every bad outcome as someone else's language problem ("the language you're using could be misinterpreted as an accusation," to Vic in E4). The show pairs the earnest man with a manager of appearances, and the two feed each other.

## How discomfort is built

- **Overlapping micro-failures.** Scenes accumulate small wrongnesses (a missing hat, a lost phone, a cup of coffee, a mistimed laugh) until a larger one lands. The tension is not resolved by a punchline; the next scene starts in the middle of the damage.
- **A hidden second layer of watchers.** The couple watch dailies, producers tell them to "cue up" scenes, and crew members laugh or fail to laugh. In E4 Dougie freezes footage to point out how uncomfortable someone is. Characters are repeatedly seen being seen. The audience is placed in the position of the producers and cannot find a clean position outside the exploitation.
- **Silence and the hold.** Many scenes end on a held reaction, not a button. In E9 a 10-second countdown clock is used as a weapon inside a marriage: the form of the game (time pressure, forced answer) is imported from reality TV into intimacy.
- **Turn-taking that fails.** Characters talk past each other in alternating reassurances. People ask "Are you defending them?" and the answer is a long hedge.
- **Mixed registers.** Social justice vocabulary ("holistic," "indigenous," "community") sits next to real-estate vocabulary (deposit, letter of intent, closing) in the same sentence, so each undermines the other.
- **Tonal whiplash.** A staged "I quit" fight (E5) is revealed as a prank on the crew; the viewer is made complicit in the trick and then in the cruelty of the reveal.

## Ethics of the camera

- The show treats filming as a power transaction. Buyers are paid to appear ($200 is floated), kids are told to stay out of shots, a baby is "not an actor," and a birthday celebration is crowbarred into a closing for a scene.
- Release forms are renegotiated whenever the network wants a "positive spin." Consent exists but is shaped by who needs the footage.
- The most direct meta-commentary: Asher sits and watches himself, being told what to feel about the edit, and his partner reviews the pilot in bed. The characters' relationship to their own image is the substance of the plot.
- The pottery scene (E9) is the cleanest moral object in the sample: a Diné artist's work is staged and filmed, honored on camera, then cut. The community's culture is a prop for the couple's goodness.
- Parallel to Fielder's non-fiction: The Rehearsal and Nathan For You also stage "real" moments, hire people to be themselves, and then ask whether the staging is exploitative or generous. The Curse takes the other side of that question, the people running the camera, and shows them as largely unable to see their own extraction.
- Background (not in the sampled transcripts): the finale's levitation sequence, widely discussed as the show's "magic" payoff, is not covered by the three available episodes.

## Contradictions

- **The curse is both real and fake.** Asher says there is no curse, but the show's structure keeps coincidence and guilt braided. The audience is not given a clean "it's psychological" answer.
- **Honest apologies that are also bids.** The earnest persona is both the show's moral center and its target.
- **Community language is sincere and instrumental at once.** Whitney does care about the town. She also schedules the care for the camera. Both statements are true, and the show refuses to rank them.
- **The couple's marriage works because of the show.** The camera exposes their problems and also supplies the only shared project that holds them together.
- **Fielder's characteristic empathy vs critique.** The show invites sympathy for Asher while documenting that he is complicit. This is more morally open than his non-fiction, where the host's good intentions are mostly left intact.
