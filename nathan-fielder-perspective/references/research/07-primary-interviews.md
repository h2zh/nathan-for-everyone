## How he answers

- **Sincere answer first, joke second, then a check-back.** In the longer settings he gives a straight, rambling, over-qualified answer and then asks whether it landed ("does that make sense?", "I'm sort of fumbling"). This shows up in the 2025 panel, the 2024 Q&A, and the NYFF Q&A. The deadpan is not constant. In low-pressure rooms he is hesitant, apologetic, self-interrupting.
- **Literalism as a weapon.** On Kimmel (2025) he treats the host's friendly question as an invitation to run a mini-experiment: he asks if there is anything Kimmel can do to put him at ease, then later asks why Kimmel did not think to offer him the slot instead of Bono. He closes with "now that we're communicating," applying his own show's thesis to the interview. The question is turned into an exercise in the thing he is promoting.
- **Takes a question at face value and then makes the interviewer's premise the topic.** CNN asks what made him look into a serious subject; he answers plainly (hobby for years, noticed one unsolved failure mode). Asked about the FAA, he is bluntly derisive ("dumb") and then immediately gives a concrete critique (the training is a slide, nothing makes it stick emotionally). This is more direct than the "never confrontational" picture.
- **Role-play inside the interview.** On CNN he accepts "First Officer Blunt / Captain All Ears" roles and uses them to say hard things. On the 2025 panel he is clear that this is the same device the show uses: a declared role lets you say true things without fear of judgment.
- **Bit-escalation to dodge or reframe praise.** Compliments (Kimmel, Jack Black, Nolan) get redirected: to the crew, to the idea that others did the work, or to a joke that raises the stakes (the stiff-actor review, the backup guest). He does not accept praise cleanly, and does not reject it with false modesty either.
- **Silence / flatness is a tool, but mostly on late night.** Late-night appearances (Conan, Kimmel) are built bits where he is the straight man to an absurd premise. In Q&As and podcasts the flatness drops.
- **Sudden sincerity on process and friends.** NYFF: "I'm not the most comforting." 2024 Q&A: he describes the "set the table" approach to actors. Both land with no joke attached.
- **When pressed on politics (2024 Q&A):** he first jokes (the Prime Minister's blackface, asks what citizenship would give him), then picks a side jokingly while the room reacts, then closes with a sincere statement that conversation across beliefs matters and that binary thinking blocks understanding. Pressure produces a bit, then a quiet earnest wrap.

## On his own process

- **Rehearsal / preparation:** In 2013 he describes himself as someone who could not read subtlety growing up and would panic socially. The show creates "a world in which subtlety can't exist for other people," so he only registers a "no" if it is explicit. In 2025 he says the point of role-play for pilots is that the role removes the fear of being judged. These are the same idea at two ends of 12 years: structured fictions as a way to say or hear what people otherwise won't.
- **Real people:** He repeatedly values the unplanned detail that real people and real places supply (the plexiglass with 2x4s in a burger shop, locals cast from parking lots, the grand-opening that matched the scripted scene). He wants his scripts to bend around what he finds. In the 2024 Q&A he says the script was adjusted after meeting locals, that actors were told mistakes are fine, and that "real" input from non-actors could change a line.
- **Ethics and consent, as stated:** Not framed as a philosophy. He mentions it as practice: the Humane Society was present for the pig stunt (2013); the Curse crew tried to be non-intrusive in Espanola, was "hyperconscious" of being intruders, and wrote that discomfort into the show. In 2013 he was "scared of the way the world worked" when a fake pig video reached the national news, rather than scared of getting in trouble. On You Can See Everything he and Oppenheim kept cameras down at times to give the family space; the NYFF account has a breakdown with the subject's partner over control and independence.
- **Failure is part of the story:** 2013 "we're fine with ideas not working out because we make that part of the story." Failed ideas get their own segment. This is a stable position from 2013 to 2026: no plan is allowed to be a precondition for the work, only the kill-switch (A24 deal) and the freedom to throw it out.
- **Anxiety and awkwardness:** Consistently owned and consistently minimized in tone. 2013: he hates being on camera and does not know why he keeps pitching himself. 2017: he enjoys wire-walking because it forces him out of his head. 2023: "I was probably most insecure going in about my acting." 2025: he says the tour of fame is odd and that he sees himself as a clown until he puts on a pilot's uniform.
- **Idea generation:** Not top-down. "I don't really want to do anything that I do, I just end up stumbling in." He starts with an anecdote or obsession (the curse story from Westwood, 20 years of plane crash hobby), tells it to a friend repeatedly, and it accretes. He describes ideas as piling up and growing, not being designed.
- **Comedy vs seriousness:** He insists both are real. CNN: "the show is still comedy, but also this is a serious thing." Kimmel: he does not want laughing at the uniform to read as laughing at pilots. Writing: "everything's written, even the reality-based stuff; you're writing in the edit."
- **Tone as the main craft object (Showtime Q&A):** He and Safdie describe tone as the star; once tone is right, other choices fall into place. He cites old Candid Camera and Columbo (using real time for ordinary actions) as models, and says discomfort ("cringe") came from letting scenes keep running past where TV normally cuts.

## How he observes others

- **Attentive to small mismatches.** He notices when people go along with an idea because they are polite (small business owners who did not want to hurt his feelings), a magician's patter being a century out of date, an HGTV couple where something is clearly wrong. He narrates these as the interesting part, not as the joke.
- **Detail over type.** He values specifics (who sits in which chair, the hoverboard, the real flavor company that cannot say no). He rarely generalizes from a person to a category. The Elizabeth Holmes account is entirely made of small moments the crew could not agree on: "what happened with the eggs."
- **Self-observation is the same method:** at NYFF he describes tracking his own thoughts around Holmes ("I know she's a liar" while she seems genuine) as a "weird mental thing." He is as interested in his own reading as in the person.
- **Generosity toward people who are not sophisticated.** He describes the sweetness of people wanting to believe a fake viral video; the characters in The Curse are described as people he feels for ("I do love Dougie") even when making bad choices; locals are given the right to say "that's not true."
- **Reads institutions as communication failures.** The FAA, Congress, local zoning: all explained in terms of who will not say the thing and who cannot hear it.
- **Gentle but pointed with powerful people.** CNN: blunt about the FAA, but on the congressman he is specific (the Congressman did not seem aware of CRM when raised) rather than rhetorical.

## Position changes over time

- **2013:** He tells Simmons that his on-screen character is a deliberately exaggerated version of his younger self and that the show is a way of making fun of it. He says he hates being on camera. He pitches the business-school/marketing world as something fun to mock because it asks "will this help business" before "does this make the world better." The tone is frank, self-effacing, slightly defensive about being unknown.
- **2017:** Fame is modest. He describes the work as grueling and the format as dense; he is still resistant to bingeing. The wire-walking story shows the "do the real thing in full" instinct (nine months of training for a small segment).
- **2023 (The Curse):** First time acting a scripted role in his own show. He names acting insecurity openly, jokes about being a limited, stiff actor and that the stiff character is "very different from the real me," while promoting that same performance. He also separates fiction from non-fiction by saying he and Benny both pursue realism from opposite ends.
- **2025 (Rehearsal S2):** Genuinely advocacy-flavored. He wants the FAA, Congress, and pilot training to adopt role-play for communication. He says he got licensed because no one would take a comedian seriously otherwise. He now speaks publicly as a partisan for an idea, not just a satirist of institutions. He is also more openly irritated at being dismissed ("they're dumb").
- **2026 (You Can See Everything):** He is more openly uncertain than before. He says he still does not understand what he went through, that he and Oppenheim disagreed about basic events, and that he is looking forward to audience reactions to get clarity. He is more willing to be filmed on camera than in earlier plans (he originally did not plan to appear). He also admits he is "not the most comforting" in a final-30-days setting. The earlier pattern of controlling the scene is replaced by a crew that deliberately tried to stay out of the way.
- **Net direction:** from "I am playing an exaggerated version of a former me" (2013) to "I am trying to figure out what is real, and I may not be able to" (2026). The guardedness softens; the interest in how people communicate, avoid saying things, and perform sincerity stays constant.

## Persona vs self

- The late-night appearances are staged: the backup guest, the Times-review gag, the "was I a hero" bit. In those he plays a hyper-earnest, slightly obtuse version of himself and treats absurd logistics as normal.
- In Q&As and podcasts the voice is similar in cadence but softer: more filler, more apology, more laughing at himself, and a habit of checking whether he made sense. This version is better described as shy, curious, and a bit disorganized, not as a cold deadpan.
- In 2013 he explicitly says the on-screen self is a version of his off-camera difficulty with social cues, amplified. This is the clearest self-account of the persona.
- On Kimmel (2023) he plays "out of character" ("this is the real me") while doing a very clearly staged bit; the line between persona and self is itself the gag.
- Lance Oppenheim (2026, IndieWire, second-hand through a close friend, not Nathan) calls the film the most vulnerable and closest to the person that he has seen, says Nathan is a strong "detector" of what people are not saying, and says people who know him only from TV underrate his emotional attunement. Treated as an observation, not as Nathan's own statement.

## Contradictions

- **Hates being on camera (2013) vs. repeatedly building projects around himself.** He acknowledges this himself ("I keep pitching projects with myself in it").
- **"Real" vs. staged.** He says the reality segments are written and edited; he also says the pig video had no plan beyond posting it. Both claims hold: scripted setups, unscripted outcomes.
- **Wants control vs. wants to be out of the way.** The Curse and Rehearsal are tightly built and rehearsed; You Can See Everything was shot with cameras deliberately kept out of the way, with no plan, and a "kill switch" rather than a script.
- **Not a hero vs. calling himself a hero.** On Kimmel he declines the word "hero" while narrating being the pilot who "saved 150 lives." On CNN he calls the flight "the miracle over the Mojave." The self-aggrandizement is deadpan, but it is also what the show's argument rests on (his flight demonstrated a communication problem).
- **Comedian vs. advocate.** He says "it's still comedy" while demanding federal action and calling a government agency dumb on CNN. He does not resolve this; he lets both stand.
- **Sincere about aviation, but jokes about his own sincerity.** He pitches the HBO budget joke ("surely HBO cares about more than entertainment") on a panel in which he is also seriously arguing for training reform.
- **Politics.** He says conversation across political lines matters, in the same session where he jokes about voting for a candidate and blackface in Canadian politics. Joking is how he approaches the topic; the sincere position appears only after.
- **"I do not want to do anything I do" vs. very high effort.** Wire-walking for nine months, 2.5 years of flight training, three-and-a-half years of footage. The stated attitude is passive; the practice is obsessive.
