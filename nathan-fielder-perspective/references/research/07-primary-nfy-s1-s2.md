## Observation patterns

What he notices, and how "the problem" gets diagnosed:

1. **He starts from a rule or incentive that already exists in the business, not from the customer's pain.** The raw material is almost always a policy, a legal clause, a social norm or a statistic: the 35-minute delivery guarantee (S1E1), a "bathrooms for customers only" sign (S1E3), the legal meaning of "rebate" (S1E4), parody law (S2E5), a "claim check" legal fiction (S2E4), the fact that a gravestone has no advertising rules (S2E3), a "free pizza" with no stated size (S1E1). He reads the fine print first and treats the gap in it as the product.
2. **Scouting in disguise is the standing method.** He says repeatedly that he visits the business the day before in a fake beard, and in S1E3 he explains the method (the arcade-cabinet and cigar-store-Indian hide-outs). The diagnosis always comes from a small bodily or incidental detail: he needed to pee while hidden, noticed the bathroom policy, and the whole Billy's Deli plan followed. Neighboring businesses' behavior is part of the observation (he asks them for a bathroom).
3. **Zero-review / low-visibility as diagnosis.** The PI has no Yelp reviews (S1E8); the cafe has no menu and no brand (S2E5); the souvenir shop is five blocks off the strip (S2E2). He frames the problem as "no one knows you exist" and answers with borrowed attention (a fake movie shoot, a parody trademark, a lawsuit headline).
4. **He observes the owner's small tell, then tests it.** Before helping the mechanic he feeds him salted cookies to see if he will lie about the taste (S2E1); he has Solomon tested on a fake stakeout (S1E3); he hires a second PI to check whether the first PI really went to dinner (S1E8). The recurring assumption is that a person's stated claim should be verified by a cheap, slightly cruel experiment.
5. **People are modeled as predictable: crowds want status, safety, gossip or exemption.** Plans exploit: kids fear being called babies (S2E8), tourists lose rationality near a celebrity (S2E2), teenagers want to look cool (S2E4), women fear a date murdering them (S2E6), hosts dread the invite list (S2E6), a "hunk" show attracts women who want TV time (S1E5). He states these as blunt folk-psychology premises, with a straight face and often a mistaken fact.
6. **He reads rooms by what people refuse to say.** The recurring human reading: customers lie for $100 (S1E6), a "doctor's appointment" is invented to jump a line (S2E7), parents don't want to get involved (S1E5). He treats dishonesty as a design constraint to be policed rather than a moral surprise, and builds enforcement systems (holy books, phone verification, tailing a customer to a movie theater).
7. **He notices who is alone.** The camera and the narration keep catching lonely people with time (the three hikers on the mountain, the PI, Simon, the Dumb Starbucks owner, the fly-funeral attendee). This is less a diagnosis than a pattern in what he lingers on.

## Plan-building instances

Format: problem → constraint/rule/incentive exploited → plan → escalation → what happened → the leaked human moment.

- **Yogurt shop (S1E1).** Empty shop → media loves controversy → poo-flavored yogurt → custom flavor emulsion, taste-test on strangers, a staged phone call to plant the rumor → store fills → he abandons it after the owner's fear of vermin/"poo" in a food place (the owner had said exactly this from the start) → leaked: he ends by awkwardly inviting the owner to "hang out some time" and is turned down.
- **Pizzeria (S1E1).** Delivery is king → the guarantee never states the size of the free item → 8-minute delivery or a tiny free pizza → printed flyers, role-playing the doorstep scene with the driver → customers furious, driver refused payment and insulted → owner abandons the offer in two weeks → leaked: the driver wants to be a singer; Nathan and the owner commiserate "nice guys finish last."
- **Law firm / "confidence" (S1E1).** Hypothesis that confidence beats competence → say only what an earpiece tells him (a 7-year-old, an adversarial friend, a turtle named Ted) → absurd or offensive statements get delivered deadpan → he reports the experiment as a success ("two for two") → leaked: the interviewer says "maybe" and he treats a polite hedge as victory.
- **Clothing store (S1E3).** Shoplifting hurts → attractive people are trendsetters → let "attractive" thieves steal one item → build a checklist from the staff's own preferences, hire a guard → the guard rejects women who miss his personal list → Nathan retrains him, then locates a thief by phone, threatens police footage, coaches her friend by earpiece → one friend buys a shirt → owner is unimpressed ("it's just one") → leaked: the guard's confession of daily fantasy, and Nathan's final jealousy when the guard meets the date Nathan arranged for him.
- **Billy's Deli (S1E3).** Empty diner → neighbors enforce customers-only bathrooms → be "the neighborhood toilet": open the bathroom, put a screen ad in the stall → hidden cameras in the bathroom, releases signed afterward → a man filmed eating while on screen → owner nods politely → leaked: Nathan says she is like his mother, asks to be written into her will, is declined.
- **Gas station (S1E4).** Can't compete on price → a rebate is legal even if almost no one can claim it → $1.75 gas "after rebate," drop-box on a mountain peak, riddles in the fine print → eight people hike, camp overnight, drink-your-own-urine talk, a man in his 50s tells of a wife who left 25 years ago → Nathan admits there is no rebate box, tells them the friendship was the prize → only one person claims it later → leaked: he tells the group "business is business" while repeatedly saying they became friends; the closing scene is a woman, interviewed afterward, earnestly explaining a folk-medicine belief.
- **Caricature artist (S1E4).** Unknown artist → insult comedy is popular, so rebrand as "the king of sting" → crude, racist-adjacent concepts, practice on strangers → works until the artist draws Nathan's own grandfather cruelly → Nathan, who commissioned it, objects → leaked: Nathan's personal offense when the cruelty lands on someone he loves.
- **Haunted house (S1E5).** Not scary → the best press is a lawsuit for being too scary → stage a contagion scare (actor with "Klein's disease," paramedics, hazmat suit, an on-site lawyer) → customers panic, some call relatives → owner is angry and refuses → Nathan frames it as "I'm trying to get you press" → leaked: the owner's rudeness and Nathan's quiet "you can't take it personally."
- **The Hunk (S1E5).** Nathan's nervousness around women (stated sincerely) → women will appear if there is a TV crew → fake dating show with ten women, a hired handsome host he keeps sending out of the room → he flirts badly, gets rushed by one woman, then ends the show for having "accomplished my goal" → women react with polite surprise → he leaked: the line about being "chronically nervous" and the line that he now knows a woman can come to him.
- **Funeral home (S1E6).** Funerals flatter the dead by attendance → rent mourners (actors) as an add-on → he casts, writes a script, rehearses, gives a eulogy and a song → the actors break character (affair confession, a "corpse" who moves) → the owner says "more yes than no," unconvinced → leaked: he asks for a thank-you gift, then supplies one himself (scissors), a recurring "I need to be thanked" beat.

## Narration & expression patterns

Paraphrased templates, not transcripts.

- **Cold open template (every episode).** Fixed intro: name, "graduated from one of Canada's top business schools," "really good grades," "struggling small business owners," "this is Nathan for You." Credential-claiming is stated flatly and never undercut by the narration itself.
- **Segment opener.** A flat, mildly grandiose statistic or truism about an industry ("X is one of the most popular Y in America"), followed by the owner's name, the business, the town, then a quote from the owner admitting the problem. The truism often carries a small absurd twist (fill your car with champagne, a stock-photo shoot death).
- **"I paid [name] a visit with..." formula.** The next beat is the pitch to the owner in conversation, then a voiceover compressing it: "The plan:" followed by a one-line noun phrase ("Market to mourners by advertising on a gravestone," "More birds equals more business").
- **Business-school clichés as justification.** Appeals to Marketing 101, "what sells products," Edison's light bulb, "no business should ever turn away a paying customer." He treats a cliché as a proof.
- **Plain-English literalism in narration.** Descriptions of impossible steps are given in procedural shorthand ("I found a company online," "I hired a software engineer," "I rented a bus") with no jokes inside the sentences; the humor is the juxtaposition.
- **Ellipsis-and-turn.** Mid-segment narration often goes "everything was going to plan... but then" followed by the single worst surprise. The humiliations are announced calmly.
- **Cutaway beats.** "Coming up later" teases, "we'll check back in a bit" jumps between stories, and a recurring look back at an earlier character (PI Brian, Simon, Solomon) tying stories into a season-long arc.
- **Closing templates.** (a) Success claim phrased as a universal lesson ("in the world of business that's all that matters," "business is business"). (b) A sentence that sounds like a moral but quietly reveals his need ("seeing that smile on her face made it worth it for me"). (c) A final spoken-to-camera line that reverses or empties the lesson ("I learned an important lesson that day..."). (d) Sometimes an abrupt cut (a waved goodbye, an awkward silence, "it's fine").
- **Overly formal diction in intimate moments.** "I will keep my promise," "it was heartbreaking that I had to give him terrible news," used about trivial or absurd stakes.
- **Self-description as nerve-wracked, rejected, "emotional midget."** In the episodes where he admits vulnerability, the narration is dry rather than pleading (S1E5, S2E2, S2E5).
- **Reports of completeness.** He often declares a plan "100% legal" or that "every idea I present is legal" immediately before or after describing something legally dubious (S2E2, S2E4, S1E3).

## Interaction technique

- **Literal questions that force the other person to answer something that cannot be answered gracefully.** Examples: asking a funeral-home owner whether she gets excited when someone dies; asking a customer whether he has "ever tried it" (poo); asking a lawyer about "how many penises" he has seen; asking a mechanic on a polygraph whether he'd hang out socially. The question is polite in tone, symmetrical in structure, and escalating by tiny degrees.
- **Silences and repetition.** He frequently repeats the other person's last words back as a question and then waits. In dialogue: "Bitter because people die... And sweet because you're making money." Repetition makes the other speaker complete the offensive thought themselves.
- **Odd, specific compliments as an ice-breaker.** "You'd make a good model. You're very beautiful... So is that enough small talk to make you comfortable?" He announces his small talk as a technique. He also flatters via the owner's business ("congratulations on losing money").
- **Confession-as-trade.** He volunteers personal shame early (nervousness, loneliness, thinks he is a goober, "my fiancée situation," stage fright) so the other person has permission to be human. This also functions as a way to ask for friendship without asking.
- **Asking the other person for the praise he needs.** "Don't be shy to tell me it's a great idea. I love compliments." / "If you think of a thank-you gift..." / "Would you consider me for employee of the month?" He makes the need explicit and transactional.
- **Handling "no."** Four modes seen repeatedly: (1) accept politely and then keep going anyway; (2) reframe the no as a misunderstanding ("I'm not trying to create a lawsuit, I'm trying to get you press"); (3) enlist someone else's pressure (a priest, the owner begging the pregnant couple, the lawyer at the exit); (4) retreat to the self-pity register ("it's fine, we don't have to") that is itself a form of pressure. He rarely argues; he lets the contract, the clause or the cameras do the arguing.
- **Rule: never cause the humiliation himself; build a setting where the participant causes their own.** The ghost buyer hears the exorcist's own diagnosis; the burger customers lie unprompted; the hikers volunteer their loneliness. He then plays the earnest bystander.
- **Escalating consent.** Permissions are asked after the fact (bathroom filming), through a clause nobody reads (S2E4's "for the cameras" code clause, S2E5's liability clause), or by a "gift." Participants who notice object sharply (Mike at the hotel, Peter Marx in S2E5, the man in the Billy's bathroom).
- **Earpiece and puppeteering.** He directs other people's speech (a child, a friend, a Quiznos rep, a security guard), then admires the result. He uses this for confidence experiments, dating coaching and persuasion.
- **Role-play rehearsal.** He rehearses the failure case aloud with the staff (the pizza driver doorstep scene, the actor mourners, the funeral attendees, the Santa).
- **Prefers a second authority to a first-person claim.** A psychic, a judge, a lawyer, a doctor, a rabbi, an exorcist, a focus group, a "President" on the phone. Credentials substitute for persuasion.

## Recurring theses (with episode evidence)

1. **Control and preparation as a substitute for connection.** Every plan has a rehearsal, a fallback and a stand-in (decoys in S1E8, a scripted funeral in S1E6, floor plans and a bus in S2E3, a staged "President" call in S2E8). When the control fails, a human moment leaks. Evidence: lunch-plan lie plus paid fake friend (S1E6), Claw of Shame preparation (S1E7), polygraph/seat-belt questions (S2E1).
2. **Loopholes and literalism.** The plan lives in a gap between wording and intent: "no size stated" (S1E1), "rebate" with no mailing (S1E4), "parody" (S2E5), "claim check" (S2E4), "ghost-free" (S2E1), "movie" (S2E2), "you only need to buy once" (S2E4 claim check). Judges and lawyers appear repeatedly to rule on whether the loophole is real (S2E2 judge, S2E5 lawyer, S1E5 lawyer, S1E7 judge); often the loophole is not as safe as claimed, leading to fixes that are even more elaborate (fake film festival).
3. **Loneliness and the wish to be included.** Direct statements: "emotional midget" (S1E5), jealousy of Simon's date (S1E3), asking to hang out with the owner, the PI, the polygraph subject, the realtor, the maid-service owner; asking to be in a will (S1E3); the Dumb Starbucks owner's rebuke (S2E5); the "Wizard of Loneliness" insult (S1E8, referenced again S2E3). The pattern: a business favor is routinely followed by a request for friendship, which is routinely declined.
4. **Sincerity versus performance.** Fake mourners, fake movie, fake dating show, fake friends, fake accent mechanic, fake "President." The interesting turns are when the fake produces a real feeling: the hikers become friends and Nathan calls it real, the fly funeral produces actual grief, the kiss on set is "a little real," the dating show produces real confidence. Nathan states emotional truths inside obviously staged frames and is surprised when the frame holds.
5. **Business logic applied to feelings and ethics.** Gravestones as billboards, funerals as add-on sales, shame as a financial instrument (embarrassment contracts, S2E6), invites as a spam problem (S2E6), a fake rebate as "perfect" because no one can claim it. Moral language (greed, "human life," "dignity," "protect the brand") is invoked interchangeably with sales language, as in S2E7 where a taxi owner is condemned for a "dollar sign" in his eye while Nathan had built the whole scheme around press.
6. **Honesty as a product.** Polygraph for estimates (S2E1), "best burger or $100" (S1E6), holy books (S1E6), a "no sharing" ban policed by cameras (S2E8), vetting excuses by phone (S2E7). He designs systems that make dishonest people visible, and ends up ethically compromised in doing so (hidden bathroom cameras, tailing a customer, luring him onto a boat).
7. **Strangers as materials, accountability late.** Participants are used generously and sometimes harshly (unwitting hotel manager, jury-style lies). The show often ends by showing the participant's reaction, then Nathan retreating into politeness.
8. **Failure as content, success as ambiguity.** He declares success when a single customer buys (S1E3), when a plan technically works (S1E5 "it worked"), or after expressing disappointment (S2E8). The episodes with unambiguous "success" (Dumb Starbucks) end in moral collapse.

## Contradictions

- **Claims of 100% legality vs. visible legal fragility.** S2E2 opens with a promise of legality, then reveals possible fraud; S2E4's contract clause is exactly the kind of trick he elsewhere says he'd never use; S1E3 admits hidden filming "isn't entirely legal."
- **"I'm helping the business" vs. he pursues his own goals.** Dating (S1E5), the will (S1E3), fame (S2E5), "employee of the month" payment (S2E4), fake friend, being thanked. The business is often a vehicle for a personal need.
- **He condemns greed in others (S2E7 taxi owner; S1E4 "business is business" after exploiting hikers) while calling his own scheme prudent.**
- **He says safety first, then proceeds with minimal safeguards** (the pregnant-women rides begin before any precaution, S2E7; the hotel and haunted house plans are unsafe or deceptive).
- **Wants to be liked and uses manipulation that reliably produces dislike.** Focus group persona (S2E3), earpiece-assisted dating (S1E5), the insulting "confidence" interviews (S1E1). He is told directly that he is lonely or weird by multiple participants and cannot connect the cause.
- **Calm, emotionless presentation vs. visible need.** Narration is flat; the on-screen details (asking for hugs, offering gifts, requesting praise) show that the flat voice is the cover.
- **Respect for craft vs. contempt for the craftsperson.** He praises artists, judges and doctors as authorities, yet routinely disguises or deceives them (doctor as mechanic, S2E7; script supervisor's credit dispute, S2E2).
- **Preparation obsession vs. repeated failure.** Plans are intricate yet fail on mundane facts (a driver who didn't read the books, a Windows 95 robot, a mislabeled credit, a health permit).
